Share-price upside if the name re-rates to the high, average or low forward P/E it traded on over the selected window, holding NTM EPS constant — so each figure is purely the multiple move (target P/E ÷ current P/E − 1), not an earnings call. Skew = upside-to-high less downside-to-low, in percentage points: positive means more room up than down. The bullet chart shows the same thing visually — the bar spans low–to–high, the dark centre line is today's price, and the orange tick is the average; all rows share one scale so they are directly comparable. That scale is set to the 85th percentile of the range widths on the page rather than the widest one (otherwise a single blown-up name would flatten every other bar), so the widest ~15% run off the edge and are marked with a ▶ chevron. Rows are tinted green where skew exceeds +10pp and red below −10pp. High / low basis. 5–95 pctile (default) takes the 95th and 5th percentile of the window, which strips the near-breakeven blow-ups where NTM EPS approaches zero and the P/E explodes — on the true-extreme basis several loss-making names print highs of 400–2,000×, which are arithmetic artifacts rather than multiples anything could re-rate to. True extremes uses the literal window high and low; it is the more faithful read for names with a clean earnings history, but expect a handful of implausible outliers to top the skew ranking. On the percentile basis today's multiple can sit outside the band (already above the 95th or below the 5th), in which case “% to high” can be negative or “% to low” positive — both are coloured by sign. A ⚠ beside a ticker means the range is distorted: its high is more than 2.5× the window median, which happens when the name passed through a near-breakeven stretch where EPS approached zero and the P/E blew up without saying anything about valuation. That affects mostly the fintech / payments names that inflected from loss-making, and it is not fixed by switching basis — treat those upside figures as unreliable and read the Name P/E chart instead. Caveat: names listed for less than the window (recent IPOs) fall back to their full available history, shown by a shorter n — their high/low reflect a shorter and often more volatile record.

How wide a band each name's forward P/E has traded in, and how jumpy it has been — two different things, both shown. Band is the headline width measure: the 10th-to-90th-percentile spread of the multiple over the window, expressed as a % of that name's own median, so a 10× stock and a 40× stock are directly comparable. CV is the classic dispersion measure (standard deviation ÷ mean). Vol is annualised volatility of daily log changes in the multiple — a name can have a wide band but drift there slowly (low Vol), or a narrow band it moves around inside constantly (high Vol). The bar plots each name's band around its own median: the shaded box spans the 10th–90th percentile, the thin whiskers reach the full min–max, the dark tick is today's multiple, and the dashed centre is the median. All rows share one scale, set to the 85th percentile of the box half-widths — not the outright widest name, which would flatten every other bar into a sliver, and not the whiskers, which would squash the typical box to a fifth of the frame. A ▶ chevron marks a box that runs past the edge (~15% of names); whiskers simply clamp at the edge, so a whisker sitting flush against the frame is itself the “reaches further” signal — the exact min/max are in each bar's tooltip. A ⚠ means the band is inflated by a near-breakeven stretch, where EPS approached zero and the P/E blew up without saying anything about valuation — that is why the fintech names dominate the wide end, and it is a data artifact rather than genuine multiple volatility. Rolling window vs calendar period. The 1Y/3Y/5Y/Max buttons look back from today; the 2024 / 2025 / 2026 YTD and 1Q26 / 2Q26 / 3Q26 QTD buttons are exact calendar slices, which is what you want for “was the band wider in 2024, or in 1Q, than it is now”. Whichever you pick drives the headline cards, the Band column and the bars. (The quarter row's 2026 YTD button is the same slice as the year row's, so both highlight together.) The Band by year / by quarter columns are always computed regardless of that choice, so the comparison stays on screen without flipping the toggle — use Comparison columns to switch between the three calendar years and this year's quarters. The endpoint figure is coloured green when the band has narrowed and red when it has widened, and the sub-sector table carries the same comparison plus the Δ band in percentage points. In quarter mode that Δ is the last complete quarter versus 1Q — deliberately not the running QTD quarter, which holds only a part-quarter of observations and would book a mechanical narrowing as a real one, and not the YTD column, which contains the others and would make the comparison circular. QTD is still shown as a column to eyeball; it is just not what gets differenced. Shorter periods bias the band narrower. This matters far more for quarters than for years. A full quarter is roughly 62 trading days and a part-quarter fewer still, against ~250 for a year: with fewer observations there is simply less time for a multiple to travel, so a quarter will print a tighter 10th–90th spread than a year even when nothing about the name has changed. Compare quarter against quarter, and year against year — never a quarter against a year — and read a small narrowing as noise rather than a signal. The per-name comparison cell tooltips carry the observation count behind every figure so you can see what each is built on. A period with fewer than 20 observations is dropped rather than shown, which is why a quarter that has only just begun returns nothing. Caveat: names listed for less than the window (recent IPOs) fall back to their full available history, shown by a shorter n — their bands cover less time and are not strictly comparable, which matters most on the Max setting; a name that listed mid-2025 shows — for 2024 and only a partial 2025. Vol is computed on the weekday series, which forward-fills market holidays (~5% of days show no change), so it is very slightly understated across the board.

This ranks each pair as a quarterly mean-reversion trade that switches sides each quarter: it goes long whichever name is cheap versus the two stocks' own historical P/E relationship and shorts the other — so it is not a fixed long/short of one name. Disc/prem now is how far the first name's P/E sits versus the second relative to their historical average (negative = first name unusually cheap, the side the trade goes long; positive = unusually rich, so it goes long the second). Hit rate (Q) is the share of quarters the trade made money, Avg / Q the mean quarterly long-short return, Cumulative the compounded result, and IC (Q) the rank correlation between the size of the gap and the next quarter's reversion. Click any pair to expand its quarter-by-quarter record. Green = cheap / made money.

Read with care: the valuation signal needs an 18-month P/E baseline and the uploaded P/E history starts mid-2021, so hit rates rest on only ~14 quarters (end-2022 to mid-2026) — a single regime, and a 70% hit over 13 quarters still carries a wide confidence band. Figures are in-sample, gross of costs, and assume FX-hedged cross-currency legs. Read this as "which pairs reverted reliably in this window," not a forward guarantee.
